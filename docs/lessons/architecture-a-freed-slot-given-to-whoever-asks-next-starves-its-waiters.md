# Architecture: A freed slot given to whoever asks next starves its waiters

**Date**: 2026-11-15
**Lesson**: A capped resource whose waiters are polled must record who waits and owe each freed unit to the oldest of them. If a newcomer may take whatever is free at the moment it asks, a client that starts its next request from the previous one's completion never lets a waiter in.

**Why**: The Browser's six-connections-per-host gate (src/fetch/network/network_context.zig, `ConnectionSlots`) kept only the slots that were taken. A transfer that found none free waited in its scheduler's list, and `pump` asked again for each waiter at the start of every turn. But a transfer's end frees its slot inside `pump`, and the script that end runs (a promise reaction, then the page's next `fetch()`) starts the next transfer before the next `pump`. That transfer asked the gate, found a slot free, and took it. So six chains of requests passed the six slots among themselves, and a seventh transfer started only when the chains stopped.

**What Happened**: html/cross-origin-embedder-policy/credentialless/fetch.https.window.js timed out (13 -> 1) in three lanes' 32-runner production sweeps in one day, and never alone, on retry or in the slower bug-finding build. Its opener runs 24 `receive()` loops through common/dispatcher/dispatcher.js's `limiter(6)`; its two popups make their own requests to the same host. While a poll took under about 18 ms the opener kept fewer than six requests in flight (24 * t / (t + 55) < 6, with dispatcher.js's 55 ms average pause between polls) and the popups got connections. Under a sweep the server answered slower than that, the limiter always had a poll ready, and the popups' requests never started: no executor ran and all twelve subtests timed out.
Two things nearly hid it:
1. The first guess was the server's listen queue (dropped SYNs cost 1 s, 3 s, 7 s). server1's counters ruled it out in one command: `ss -ltn` showed a backlog of 2,000 and `nstat` showed `TcpExtListenOverflows` 0.
2. The first page-level replica passed on the unfixed runner. It awaited each `send()` before `receive()`, so only a few polls ran at the start and the popups loaded before the six slots filled. The upstream file does not await `send()`. With that one line as upstream has it, the replica timed out 5 of 5 on the unfixed runner and passed 13 of 13 on the fix.

**Fix**:
1. `ConnectionSlots.release` hands the freed slot, under the context's lock, to the destination's oldest waiter, which holds it from that moment; the waiter's scheduler hears so at its next pump and starts it. A transfer started in between never finds the slot free. Waiters are kept oldest first across the Browser's agents; a cancelled waiter leaves the queue, and a waiter cancelled after it was handed a slot gives it to the next.
2. The order is the browsers'. Chromium binds a released socket to its group's next pending request at once (net/socket/transport_client_socket_pool.cc: ReleaseSocket, OnAvailableSocketSlot, ProcessPendingRequest). Gecko dispatches from pending queues kept in arrival order within a priority (netwerk/protocol/http/PendingTransactionQueue.cpp, InsertTransactionSorted; it keeps one queue per window and serves the focused window's first, which Crane, with no focused-window notion in its network layer, does not copy).
3. What is still not a browser's is said in the gate's comment, not patched over: a transfer moves only when its own agent's scheduler pumps, so an agent in a long task keeps what it holds - its transfers in flight (true before this change) and now the slots its waiters were handed. I first "fixed" that with a rule that a waiter not asked about for 100 ms holds no slot back. The integrator rejected it: a timing heuristic no browser has, covering a cause I had already named (the waiter cannot start without its agent's thread). The cause's fix is networking with a thread of its own, which is an architecture item, queued; a timeout at the symptom is not a design.
4. Tests that assert order, not time: tests/fetch/network_scheduler_test.zig counts the transfers given a slot while an older waiter waited (60 before, 0 after); src/fetch/network/network_context.zig's tests read who holds a slot straight after a release (red with the hand-off line disabled: 2 of 483 fail); crane/flk1-connection-slot-order.html counts the chained requests that ended before the waiting one (60 before, under 18 after); crane/flk1-dispatcher-popups-slow-server.window.js is the upstream file's shape with every poll answered 100 ms late.

**Takeaway**: **A wait queue that is only polled is not a queue: the gate must know who waits and hand each freed unit to the oldest at the moment it is freed. When the hand-off exposes a limit of the architecture, name the limit and queue its real fix - do not add a timeout. To reproduce a starvation, copy the original's concurrency exactly: one extra `await` in the replica removed the load that caused it.**
